Casal de Colònies — booking management for a summer camp

Built for a camp in Colònia de Sant Pere, Mallorca · in production since 2025

The problem

A small camp managed its school group bookings for each season by hand. Agreements, annexes, deadlines and occupancy were spread over documents and email.

The solution

A portable desktop app that runs from a USB stick on any Windows machine, with no server and no installation. It manages schools and bookings and tracks the status of each booking: agreement sent, waiting for Annex 1, confirmed. It generates the booking agreement and Annex 1 as PDFs and emails them through the client's Gmail account, shows an occupancy calendar and upcoming deadlines, and includes a first-run checklist and a built-in manual.

The technical challenge

The app had to be portable and work offline. Electron packages a Next.js app, and the data lives in a local SQLite database through sql.js. Authentication is dual: Google sign-in when online and “continue offline” when not. Google OAuth had to work from a desktop app, and a documented migration path covers legacy data.

PDFs the client controls

The documents are the heart of the app, and the camp owns them. It uploads its own PDF templates, maps each field to booking data, and can create new fields or edit the PDF itself whenever a contract changes. The app validates that every required field is present before it generates a document, so nothing goes out half-filled.

Delivered end to end

Requirements, delivery and support for a real client, not a demo.

Working with AI

Over the last two years most of my learning has gone into one question: how does a real team ship with AI agents without losing control? This is the path so far.

  1. 01

    Assistants in the editor

    Autocomplete and chat first. Great for boilerplate and tests, unreliable on a very large ExtJS codebase it knew nothing about. First lesson: the model is only as good as the context it gets.

  2. 02

    Agents, models, side by side

    Ran the same tickets through Codex, Claude Code and OpenCode, and routed many models through OpenRouter to compare cost and quality. Added Jev, a non-LLM decision model, for routing and classification where a chatbot is the wrong tool.

  3. 03

    A harness for the whole team

    Another team started a company meta-repo; the Knowledge team adopted it and I drive most of what it became for us: role commands (/support-, /po-, /dev-) that Claude Code runs, Azure DevOps and the repos reached through MCP, and OpenSpec as the single store of behaviour — 10 capabilities and 107 requirements that 14 repositories follow.

What I've learned

  • Context beats model. The same model is useless or excellent depending on what it is handed: specs, conventions, a map of the repos.
  • Reads are free, writes ask. Every command may read anything; every write shows a numbered list and waits for a yes. Nobody fears the tool, and everything it touches is marked ai-assisted.
  • Not every call needs an LLM. Routing and classification run faster and cheaper on a decision model; generation is for text.
  • Measure on real tickets. One command, one real ticket per sprint, and a feedback file in the repo. Demos prove nothing.
